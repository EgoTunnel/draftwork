Draftwork Privacy

Instructor guide

One-page printable versions: for instructors · for students

Draftwork doesn’t decide who wrote something. It gives you a record of how the writing came together and points you to a few moments worth asking about. The conversation is what tells you whether a student did the work.

1. Set up the assignment

  1. Open Review → Assignments and create one. Pasting from other sources is blocked by default; students can still move their own text, and quote sources through the sources panel.
  2. Click Instructions for students and paste the text into the assignment in your course site. It has the link and the steps for saving and uploading, in the same words students see in Draftwork. Ready to submit gives students two files, a .draftwork file and a Word document, so choose what students should upload: both, so you can see the work in your LMS’s preview (Canvas SpeedGrader, for example), or the .draftwork file alone. The text changes to match, so students know exactly what to do. Set the dropbox to accept those file types, and two files per student if you ask for both. If you add the dropbox’s link when creating the assignment, students get a button to open it right after saving.
  3. Back up to a folder. Your assignments and check-in notes are kept only in this browser, at this web address. Clearing browsing data, or using another browser or computer, won’t have them. When you create your first assignment in Chrome or Edge, Draftwork offers to back up automatically to a folder you choose: pick one in your college OneDrive and it’s saved there whenever anything changes, with one copy per day to go back to. On another computer, choose the same folder and everything comes across. In other browsers, use Download backup and open the file wherever you need it. Draftwork shows a reminder whenever something isn’t backed up.
  4. Working with a co-teacher or TA? Use Share with a colleague to send them a file with the assignments (and, if you’re reviewing together, your check-in notes). When they open it, it’s merged with what they have: nothing is lost, and notes on the same student are combined.
  5. Tell students up front: their writing process is recorded, they can see exactly what you’ll see with Preview, and a pattern can only ever lead to a conversation, never directly to a penalty.
  6. Collect the files through your LMS dropbox as usual. The Word document is a convenience copy: a student can edit it after saving, and Draftwork doesn’t check it. The .draftwork file is the record.

2. Open the submissions

Drop the files, or the zip your LMS gives you for “download all submissions”, onto the Submissions page. Nothing is uploaded; files open in your browser. Use the filters to start with flagged work, but skim a few unflagged ones too: you’ll learn what normal looks like for your class.

3. Read a submission

  • Large class? Each assignment has a summary bar: click red, amber or green to see just those. Sort → Most serious first puts red at the top, and Previous and Next follow that order, so you can work down the list.
  • The submissions list is colour-coded: green for no flags, amber for one or two minor flags, red for a major flag (such as text typed straight through like copying, or an altered file) or three or more flags. Green is not a verdict; skim a few of those too.
  • File integrity checks only whether the file is an unaltered record made in Draftwork. “Unaltered” doesn’t mean the work is approved, or that the student wrote it: that’s what the flags are for. A failure (“Altered”) means the file was changed or made outside Draftwork. That is a real finding, but still talk to the student first: ask them to show you how they saved it.
  • Flags are reasons to ask, not conclusions. Each says in plain words what happened, with the exact measurements in small print underneath, and lists innocent explanations. Read the student’s own notes and responses beside them.
  • Writing process shows how the text grew. In composed writing the lines separate (typing, deleting, going back); copied text tends to go in as one smooth line.
  • Questions to ask are specific moments, flagged ones first. Click a question to see that moment in the replay before you ask it. If there’s time, More questions has a few others.
  • Replay plays the writing back and keeps up with where it’s happening. Scroll the page freely while it plays; Follow writing brings you back.

4. Run the five-minute check-in

Ask two or three of the suggested questions. Someone who wrote the piece answers in seconds; they remember why they cut a paragraph or moved a quote. Someone who copied it has nothing to remember.

“At 0:42 you cut 180 words from your second section. What was wrong with it?”
“This section went in with almost no changes. Did you plan it somewhere else first?”
  • Ask openly and neutrally. Don’t say what was flagged until they’ve answered.
  • Accept ordinary answers: “I outlined it on paper”, “I compose in my head”, “I was checking my notes in another tab”. Those are common and fine.
  • Be especially careful with multilingual writers, students using dictation or assistive technology, and fast typists. Linear writing is not evidence of anything on its own.
  • Record the answer and an outcome under each question, and mark each flag “benign” or “concern”. Print summary gives you a one-page record.

5. Afterwards

Follow your institution’s academic integrity process for anything that remains a concern. Draftwork’s record supports that conversation; it doesn’t replace it.

If you’re willing, share the Export → Anonymised spreadsheet with the project. Real outcomes are how the flags get better.

For students

Write the way you normally would. Draft, delete, move things around: that’s what a real writing record looks like. If something about your process is unusual (paper drafts, dictation, writing in another language first), say so in File → Notes. When you paste a quote from somewhere else, a small box asks where it’s from; filling it in saves you citing it from memory later. Before you submit, open Preview; if anything is flagged, you can add a note explaining it.
